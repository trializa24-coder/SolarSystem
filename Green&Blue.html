Understanding the Concepts

What Is the Blue & Green Curriculum?

Two curriculum innovations born from the same concern: we are teaching students many things, but not yet teaching them enough to care for the Earth.

🌊 Blue Curriculum

The Ocean Enters the Classroom

The Blue Curriculum is an educational approach that integrates ocean literacy into learning, both formal and non-formal. The idea is simple: the ocean covers 71% of the Earth’s surface, yet it is rarely addressed as a main topic in schools.

This curriculum encourages students to understand how the ocean regulates climate, supports biodiversity, and connects communities around the world. Its principle is clear: every learner deserves to be ocean-literate, not just those who live near the coast.

📜 Origins

It developed through the UN Ocean Decade and UNESCO’s work in ocean literacy. First introduced globally in the early 2000s, it has become increasingly relevant leading up to the 2030 Agenda.

🌿 Green / Greening Curriculum

Sustainability as a Way of Thinking

The Green Curriculum is not a new subject. It is a new way of looking at all existing subjects. Science, mathematics, and even social studies can be taught through a lens of sustainability and environmental awareness.

Grounded in Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) supported by UNESCO, this approach shifts classroom questions from “What is this?” to “What does this mean for the future of our planet?” The classroom is no longer just a place to absorb information, but a starting point for action.

📜 Origins

It is rooted in the ESD movement that began at the Rio Earth Summit in 1992, later reinforced through UNESCO’s Decade of Education for Sustainable Development (2005–2014), and continued within the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) agenda.

Connection to Our Topic

The Solar System & Environmental Awareness

When we learn about the Solar System, we are actually learning how rare life is in the universe and how precious the Earth we stand on truly is.

🌍
🌍

Earth — The Only One with Oceans

Of the eight planets in the Solar System, only Earth has liquid oceans on its surface. This is not just trivia it is one of the strongest reasons why we must protect the ocean. The Blue Curriculum teaches us that the ocean is not merely a background on a map, but a life-supporting system that no other planet possesses.

🌱

The Habitable Zone — Earth Lies Right at the Edge

Earth lies within the “Goldilocks Zone”—the ideal distance from the Sun that allows liquid water and life to exist. However, human activities that damage the environment are gradually disrupting this balance. The Green Curriculum encourages students to understand that protecting Earth’s climate is not about trends, but about preserving the conditions that make life possible.

☀️

The Sun & Natural Cycles

Solar energy drives the water cycle, weather patterns, and photosynthesis on Earth. When we learn about the Sun within the Solar System topic, we are indirectly learning why solar energy is one of the most powerful solutions to the climate crisis, something that directly aligns with the values of the Green Curriculum.

🔭

A Space Perspective

Astronauts who see Earth from space often experience the “Overview Effect,” a profound sense of awe at how small and fragile our planet is. This is the perspective that the Blue and Green Curriculum aim to cultivate: seeing Earth not as a resource to be exploited, but as a home to be protected.

Real Cases & Global Issues

Why This Matters?

The data below are not just numbers. They reflect the real consequences of an education system that has not yet taken environmental issues seriously enough.

71%
Most of Earth’s surface is covered by oceans, yet ocean-related topics are largely absent from school curricula in many countries.
1.5°C
The threshold for critical global temperature rise. The generation learning today is the one that will face it tomorrow.

17.000+
Islands in Indonesia—making ocean and environmental literacy not only globally relevant, but also deeply personal and urgent for us.
🌏 Real Case in Indonesia

Environmental issues are not abstract concepts. In Indonesia, coastal flooding (banjir rob) in cities such as Semarang and Jakarta has become more frequent due to rising sea levels and land subsidence. At the same time, Indonesia is among the largest contributors to marine plastic waste globally, with millions of tons entering the ocean each year.

These cases demonstrate that the challenges addressed by the Blue and Green Curriculum are already happening around us. Therefore, integrating ocean literacy and sustainability into education is not optional, but essential for preparing students to respond to real-world environmental problems.

Indonesian Curriculum Context

Connection to the Indonesian Curriculum

Many elements of the Blue and Green Curriculum already exist within our education system, but they have not yet been fully developed or implemented effectively.

🏛️ At the Policy Level

The Merdeka Curriculum (2022) introduces significant changes. One of the themes in the Pancasila Student Profile Strengthening Project (P5) is explicitly “Sustainable Lifestyle,” which directly aligns with the principles of the Green Curriculum that encourage students to take real action toward their environment.

At the national level, Indonesia has also committed to the UN 2030 Agenda, and the Ministry of Education has begun integrating ESD principles into the national education framework. However, for ocean literacy in particular, a systematic integration is still lacking, and this is where the Blue Curriculum has significant room to be introduced.

📚 Koneksi ke Kurikulum IPA

Science → Blue Curriculum
Topics such as ecosystems, the water cycle, and biodiversity are already included in science education, but they are rarely framed from an ocean literacy perspective. The Blue Curriculum encourages this shift in framing.
Science → Green Curriculum
Topics such as climate change, energy, and environmental degradation in science align directly with the goals of the Green Curriculum: learning about nature in order to take action for it.
Solar System → Both
The Solar System topic already included in the science curriculum can serve as a powerful entry point, teaching students that Earth is the only habitable planet we know, making its protection not a choice, but a necessity.
Gaps & Opportunities
Most of this content is still taught as information, not as a call to action. The shift from “knowing about the environment” to “acting for the environment” is exactly what these two curricula offer.
Implementation in Education

How to Get Started?

Change doesn't always require a revolution. It can start with one class, one teacher, one small project, and spread from there.

👩‍🏫 For Teachers

Frame your lesson review

  • Connect science topics to local environmental issues, such as coral bleaching, tidal flooding, or deforestation.
  • Design a P5 project with an ocean or sustainability theme
  • Use real data: local river water quality, remaining forest area, average annual temperature
  • Encourage students to ask deeper questions: "Why is this happening? And what can we do about it?"
💡 Examples of Solar System Topics

When teaching about Earth's position in the solar system, include a discussion about the Goldilocks Zone and why Earth's conditions are so unique. Follow up by asking, "What would happen if Earth's temperature continued to rise?" and link it to climate change and concrete actions.

🧑‍🎓 For Students

Be an Agent of Change

  • Start an eco-audit at your school, how much waste is produced per day?
  • Document changes in the environment around you through photos or journals.
  • Turn science assignments into real proposals for the community
  • Join or form an environmental or marine awareness club at school.
💡 Inspiration from Outer Space

Astronauts say seeing Earth from space changes the way they view life. You don't have to go to space to feel that; just study photos of Earth from orbit and ask yourself, "What do I want to preserve about this planet?"

🌏 For Society

Small Actions, Real Impact

  • Reduce single-use plastic, especially near rivers and oceans.
  • Take part in beach or river cleanup activities in your city.
  • Support schools that implement the Adiwiyata program
  • Keep learning: read about the progress of SDGs 14 and 15 in Indonesia
🏫 For School

Build a Green & Blue Culture

  • List of Adiwiyata programs for sustainability to become part of the school's identity
  • Integrate marine topics into various subjects, not just science
  • Establish collaborations with environmental NGOs or local research institutions
  • Create a school garden, compost system, or rainwater harvester as a real-life project.
