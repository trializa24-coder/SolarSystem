Our Solar System

Eight planets, one star, and a universe of mysteries. Think like a NASA scientist — explore, question, and discover the wonders of our cosmic neighborhood.

🔬 Guided Inquiry Activity
🌍 Real-World Situation

It's 2030. You are a young scientist working at NASA. Your mission: help find the best planet for humans to live on — after Earth. Climate change has made parts of Earth harder to live in. Scientists around the world are studying other planets. Your job is to look at the data and decide: which planet could be our next home?

🌡️

Surface Temperature
Some planets are too hot. Some are too cold. Can any be just right?

💨

Atmosphere
We need air to breathe! Does the planet have any gases we can use?

🌊

Water
No water, no life. Which planets might have liquid water — now or in the past?

🤔 Think about this: If you had to choose one planet (other than Earth) to build a human colony, which one would you pick? Why? Write your first thoughts below before you start learning!

A good scientist always starts with a clear question. Read the three questions below. Pick the one you are most curious about. You will try to answer this question at the end of the activity!

A.
Which planet in our Solar System is the most likely place for humans to live in the future?
You will compare planets using real NASA data — temperature, gravity, atmosphere, and distance from Earth.
B.
Why do different planets have such different temperatures, even though they all get energy from the same Sun?
You will investigate how distance from the Sun, atmosphere, and planet size affect surface temperature.
C.
How does a planet's size and mass affect the gravity that living things would feel on its surface?
You will look at the relationship between planet mass, diameter, and surface gravity across all 8 planets.

A hypothesis is your best guess before you start investigating. It's not about being right or wrong — it's about making a prediction based on what you already know. A good hypothesis uses the format: "I think... because..."

💡

Use "I think... because..."
Example: "I think Mars is best because I've seen it on the news."

📖

Use what you already know
It's okay to use everyday knowledge — no research needed yet!

✏️

Be specific
Mention which planet or which factor your hypothesis is about.

Write at least 2 sentences. Use "I think... because..." to start!

Time to investigate! 🚀 Like a real NASA scientist, you will collect information by reading the planet data below. Complete each task to build your evidence.

1
📋 Read the Planet Cards
Scroll down and click on each planet card below. Read the description and data. Pay attention to: temperature, gravity, moons, distance from Earth, and atmosphere. You don't need to read all 8 — focus on the planets most related to your research question.
2
📝 Take Notes on 3 Planets
After reading, come back here and fill in what you found about your 3 most interesting planets. Write in the boxes below.
3
🤔 Compare and Think
Look at the numbers. Which planet seems best for your question? Does your evidence match your hypothesis, or did you find something surprising?
💡 Tip: You don't need to know everything! Real scientists always find things they didn't expect. It's perfectly okay to change your mind after reading the data.

Great work, scientist! 🎉 Now it's time to write your conclusion. A conclusion answers your research question using evidence (facts you found). Look back at your notes from Step 4 and write your final answer.

Try to use facts and data from the planet cards to support your answer. Aim for at least 3 sentences.

Use these starters: "Before this, I thought..." / "Now I know..." / "I am still wondering about..."
