Core (The Centre)
Deepest Layer
The core is the very centre of the Sun — the most powerful place in our Solar System. Here, hydrogen atoms are squeezed together so hard that they join to make helium, and this releases a huge amount of energy. This is the same process as a hydrogen bomb, but happening non-stop for billions of years! All the light and heat the Sun gives us comes from the core.
Temperature
15 Million °C
Size
~20% of Sun
Density
150 g/cm³
📚 Key Word
Nuclear Fusion: When two hydrogen atoms join together and release a massive burst of energy and light.
✨
Radiative Zone
Energy Travel
After energy is made in the core, it slowly travels outward through this zone. The energy moves as tiny packets of light (photons). But it's very crowded here — the photons keep bouncing off atoms and changing direction. It can take up to 170,000 years for energy to travel through this zone! That's longer than human civilization has existed.
From
20% → 70%
Travel Time
~170,000 yrs
📚 Key Word
Photon: A tiny particle of light. The photons made in the core take thousands of years to reach the surface!
🌀
Convection Zone
Boiling Gas
Think of this like a boiling pot of soup. Hot gas rises up to the surface, cools down, then sinks back — over and over again. You can actually see the top of these bubbles on the Sun's surface! They are called granules. This churning motion also creates the Sun's powerful magnetic field, which causes sunspots and solar flares.
From
70% → Surface
Bubble Size
~1,000 km each
📚 Key Word
Convection: When hot liquid or gas rises and cool liquid or gas sinks — like boiling water in a pot.
💡
Photosphere
The Visible Surface
This is the layer we can actually see — the bright, glowing surface of the Sun. When you look at the Sun in a photo (never look directly!), this is what you see. The photosphere is where sunspots appear — dark patches that are slightly cooler than the surrounding area. Even though they look dark, sunspots are still incredibly hot — around 3,500°C!
Temperature
5,778 K
Thickness
~500 km
📚 Key Word
Sunspot: A darker, cooler area on the Sun's surface caused by strong magnetic fields blocking heat from below.
🔴
Chromosphere
Lower Atmosphere
Just above the surface is the chromosphere — a thin, pinkish-red layer of the Sun's atmosphere. You can only see it during a total solar eclipse, when it appears as a red ring around the Moon's shadow. Giant loops of hot gas called prominences shoot out from here, stretching hundreds of thousands of kilometers — bigger than the entire Earth!
Temperature
4,000–25,000 K
Thickness
~2,000 km
📚 Key Word
Prominence: A huge arch of glowing gas that shoots out from the Sun's surface, shaped by magnetic forces.
👑
Corona
Outer Atmosphere
The corona is the Sun's outer atmosphere — a giant glowing halo stretching millions of kilometers into space. Here's a big mystery scientists are still studying: the corona is much hotter than the surface (1–3 million °C vs 5,778 K)! The corona is also where the solar wind comes from — streams of particles that flow out to all the planets.
Temperature
1–3 Million K
Extends
Millions of km
📚 Key Word
Solar Wind: A stream of tiny particles that flows out from the corona in all directions — it reaches Earth and beyond!

Solar Events

Amazing Things the Sun Does

🌑
Sunspots

Dark patches on the Sun's surface that are slightly cooler than the surrounding area. They form where strong magnetic fields stop hot gas from rising up. Sunspots come and go in an 11-year cycle — when there are more sunspots, the Sun is more active.

🔥
Solar Flares

Sudden, powerful bursts of energy and light from the Sun's surface. They happen when magnetic field lines get twisted and then suddenly snap back. A big solar flare can affect radio communication and GPS satellites on Earth!

🌊
Prominences

Giant arches of glowing gas that rise from the chromosphere and loop back into the corona. They can be hundreds of thousands of kilometers long — much bigger than Earth! Some of them last for months before disappearing.

🌌
Solar Wind & Auroras

The Sun constantly sends out a stream of tiny charged particles called solar wind. When these particles hit Earth's magnetic field, they create the beautiful Northern and Southern Lights (auroras) that glow in the sky near the poles!

Predict & Connect · Activity 5

You read that auroras (Northern/Southern Lights) happen when solar wind particles hit Earth's magnetic field.

Predict: If the Sun sent out a much stronger solar wind than usual, what do you think would happen to the auroras? Would they get bigger, smaller, or disappear? Explain your reasoning.


Well predicted! A stronger solar wind carries more charged particles, which means more particles hit Earth's magnetic field. The result? Auroras become much brighter, more colorful, and visible from areas much farther from the poles than usual — sometimes even visible from countries near the equator during extreme solar storms. This actually happened during the famous Carrington Event in 1859, one of the largest solar storms ever recorded!
Celestial Events

Types of Solar Eclipse

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon moves between Earth and the Sun, blocking the Sun's light. There are three different types — it all depends on how much of the Sun gets covered!

Total Eclipse
Total Solar Eclipse

The Moon completely covers the Sun. The sky goes dark in the middle of the day! You can see the corona glowing around the Moon like a halo. This only lasts a few minutes and only happens in a narrow path on Earth.

Partial Eclipse
Partial Solar Eclipse

The Moon covers only part of the Sun, so the Sun looks like it has a bite taken out of it! This is the most common type. Always use special eclipse glasses — never look directly!

Annular Eclipse
Annular Solar Eclipse

The Moon is a bit farther from Earth, so it looks smaller and creates a beautiful "ring of fire" — a bright ring of sunlight visible around the edge of the Moon!

Why the Sun Matters

The Sun's Role in Life on Earth

Without the Sun, there would be no life on Earth — no plants, no animals, no humans. Here's how the Sun supports every living thing on our planet:

🌱
Photosynthesis

Plants use sunlight to make their own food from carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water. This process — called photosynthesis — also produces the oxygen we breathe! Without the Sun, there would be no plants, and without plants, there would be no animals or humans either.

🌡️
Climate & Seasons

The Sun heats Earth's surface unevenly — more at the equator, less at the poles. This creates wind, weather patterns, and ocean currents that move heat around the planet. The angle of sunlight also causes the four seasons as Earth orbits the Sun.

💧
The Water Cycle

Solar heat evaporates water from oceans, lakes, and rivers — turning liquid water into water vapour that rises into the atmosphere. Up in the sky it cools and forms clouds, then falls back as rain or snow. This cycle keeps our freshwater supplies full!

⚡
Solar Energy

We can capture sunlight using solar panels and turn it into electricity — clean energy with no pollution! In fact, wind power, hydropower, and even fossil fuels are all forms of stored solar energy. The Sun is the original energy source for almost everything!

🧬
Vitamin D & Health

When sunlight touches your skin, your body makes Vitamin D — an essential nutrient that helps build strong bones and keep your immune system healthy. This is why a little sunshine every day is actually good for you!

🌍
Keeping Earth in Orbit

The Sun's enormous gravity holds the entire Solar System together. It keeps Earth in the Goldilocks Zone — where temperatures are just right for liquid water to exist and life to survive.

Big Picture Thinking · Activity 8

Imagine that the Sun's energy output suddenly dropped by half for one year.

Using what you've learned about the Sun's role — list at least 3 specific things you think would change on Earth, and explain why each one would happen.


There are many correct answers! Here are some likely effects:

🌱 Plants would struggle to photosynthesize — less light means less food production, which would affect animals and humans in the food chain.
🌡️ Global temperatures would drop sharply — leading to a kind of "mini ice age." Oceans would cool, glaciers would grow.
💧 The water cycle would slow down — less evaporation means less rain, which could cause droughts and freshwater shortages.
🌬️ Wind patterns would change — since the Sun drives uneven heating that creates wind, less energy would disrupt global weather systems.

The Sun affects virtually everything — it really is the engine of life on Earth!
